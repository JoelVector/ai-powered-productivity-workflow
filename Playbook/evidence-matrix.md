## My Assessment

The most useful contribution from Gotch's research is not a specific AI tool. It is the production logic that emerges across his recommendations:

**Data + Company Knowledge + First-Hand Expertise**

↓

**SERP + Competitor + AI-Citation Research**

↓

**Entity, Topic, and Question Extraction**

↓

**Gap Analysis**

↓

**Differentiation**

↓

**Content Brief**

↓

**AI-Assisted Production**

↓

**Human Review and Optimization**

↓

**Internal Linking**

This sequence addresses one of the central risks of AI-powered SEO content production: using AI too early.

If an LLM is asked to generate an article before proprietary knowledge, competitor evidence, search intent, topic gaps, and differentiation have been established, the model is likely to produce competent but generic content.

I therefore adopt Gotch's research-first principle while keeping the final workflow tool-agnostic.


## Source-Skepticism Note

Nathan Gotch is commercially involved with Rankability, an SEO content optimization platform, and some of his educational material demonstrates workflows using his own software.

That commercial relationship does not automatically invalidate the underlying SEO principles. However, it does mean that tool-specific recommendations should not be accepted without separating the principle from the product being promoted.

For example:

**Principle accepted:** Analyze relevant entities and topic coverage before publishing.

**Vendor-specific rule rejected:** Every team must use Rankability to perform that analysis.

The final playbook will specify the capability required at each stage rather than make a particular commercial SEO platform mandatory unless there is evidence that the tool itself is uniquely necessary.


## What Nathan Gotch Contributes to the Final Playbook

At this stage of the research, I would carry forward the following principles:

1. Build topical depth instead of publishing disconnected articles.
2. Structure supporting content around strategically important core pages.
3. Research both traditional search competitors and AI-cited sources.
4. Give AI proprietary company knowledge and first-hand expertise before asking it to produce content.
5. Conduct content-gap analysis before drafting.
6. Add a deliberate differentiation step after competitor analysis.
7. Optimize for topic and intent coverage rather than arbitrary word count.
8. Preserve technical and on-page SEO fundamentals.
9. Build internal linking into the production workflow rather than treating it as an afterthought.
10. Measure AI-search visibility alongside, rather than instead of, traditional organic-search performance.

## My Assessment

Matt Diggity contributes two particularly useful ideas to this playbook.

The first is that AI-era content production should be organized around topical coverage and user questions rather than isolated keywords.

The second is that much of the mechanical work surrounding SEO production can potentially be automated.

However, I accept these two ideas to different degrees.

I strongly accept the topical-authority principle.

I only conditionally accept aggressive automation.

The reason is that AI changes the economics of content production. Generating another article is becoming extremely cheap. Verification, proprietary insight, differentiation, and editorial judgment are not becoming equally cheap.

Therefore, maximizing the number of generated articles is not automatically the same as maximizing SEO performance.

My version of the workflow will use automation to remove repetitive work, but automation will stop before an unverified piece of content is published.


## Source-Skepticism Note

Diggity frequently presents results from tests conducted on his own sites or client sites.

This is useful practitioner evidence, but several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, case-study improvements do not prove that one individual tactic caused the entire improvement. SEO performance can be affected simultaneously by backlinks, technical changes, domain authority, brand demand, content changes, algorithm updates, seasonality, and other variables.

Second, some of Diggity's posts contain large performance claims. These figures are useful as examples but will not be treated as expected outcomes for users of this playbook.

Third, Diggity has commercial relationships with SEO companies and tools. For example, he has publicly discussed being an investor in Surfer. Tool recommendations therefore require the same separation between principle and vendor that was applied to Nathan Gotch.

For this playbook:

**Principle accepted:** Automate repetitive tasks when the process is stable and the output can be validated.

**Universal claim rejected:** Automation will produce the same revenue or traffic improvements reported in a particular case study.

**Principle accepted:** Analyze topical and content gaps.

**Vendor-specific rule rejected:** Every team must use a particular SEO platform to perform that analysis.


## A Claim I Am Deliberately Not Turning Into a Rule

Some of Diggity's material makes strong claims about the behavior of AI-search systems, including statements about what AI systems "prefer," "ignore," or "rely heavily on."

I would not convert those statements into universal rules without corroboration.

AI-search products change rapidly, different systems use different retrieval architectures, and observed correlations from client sites do not necessarily reveal the internal mechanism responsible for the result.

Therefore, this playbook will prefer recommendations that remain useful even if a specific AI platform changes.

For example:

"Write clear, direct answers because they improve information retrieval and user comprehension"

is more defensible than:

"Write direct answers because every AI system always prefers them."


## What Matt Diggity Contributes to the Final Playbook

At this stage of the research, I would carry forward the following principles:

1. Build topic clusters rather than producing isolated keyword articles.
2. Use competitor content gaps as discovery inputs, not as an automatic publishing list.
3. Research conversational questions alongside traditional keywords.
4. Structure content so important sections can stand independently.
5. Answer important questions directly before expanding on them.
6. Prefer natural semantic coverage over mechanical keyword repetition.
7. Add first-hand expertise, case studies, and proprietary evidence whenever possible.
8. Automate repeatable research and production tasks where outputs can be validated.

10. Track AI-search visibility alongside conventional SEO performance.

This makes Solis useful not only for determining how content should be optimized,

## Primary Evidence

### AS-01 to AS-04 — Start With the Journey, Establish a Baseline, and Diagnose the Gap

Solis' AI Search Optimization Checklist begins before content creation.

The process first identifies the prompts and journeys the business actually wants
to influence, establishes current AI-search presence, diagnoses the visibility
gap, and then evaluates whether priority pages are sufficiently retrievable and
extractable.

This is important because it reverses a common AI-content workflow:

**Weak workflow:**

Generate content → publish → hope visibility improves

**Solis-informed workflow:**

Define desired visibility → measure baseline → diagnose gap → choose intervention

This means content creation is one possible solution, not the automatic solution
to every visibility problem.

This distinction is central to this playbook.

The question should not be:

**"Can we rank for this keyword?"**

**"Do we have a legitimate business and expertise reason to become one of the

## Primary Evidence

### KI-01 to KI-04 — The Fan-Out Effect and AI Citation Research

Research conducted by AirOps in partnership with Kevin Indig analyzed 16,851
queries and 353,799 pages across ChatGPT's retrieval and citation process.

The study found that retrieval position had a strong relationship with citation
probability.

A page appearing first in the retrieval results had a reported citation rate
of 58%, compared with 14% for a page appearing in position ten.

This provides strong evidence that conventional retrieval and SEO visibility
remain important inputs to AI-search visibility.

The study also found a significant relationship between query-aligned headings
and citations.

Pages with headings closely aligned with the user's query were cited 41% of
the time compared with 29% for pages with weaker heading alignment.

Most importantly for this playbook, exhaustive topical coverage did not
automatically perform best.

Pages covering approximately 26–50% of the fan-out subqueries outperformed
pages attempting to cover all identified subqueries.

This challenges the assumption that the optimal AI-search article should always
be the most comprehensive article possible.

I am introducing a distinction that will guide the production system:

## Topic Depth

How comprehensively the WEBSITE covers a strategically relevant subject.

## Page Depth

How much information ONE PAGE contains about a specific information need.

The playbook will aim for:

**HIGH TOPIC DEPTH**

but

**VARIABLE PAGE DEPTH**

depending on the user's intent.


## Example

**The Ultimate 10,000-Word Guide to Employee Onboarding**

- What is employee onboarding?

- Remote employee onboarding
- Employee onboarding software
- Onboarding automation
- Employee onboarding metrics
- Employee onboarding mistakes
- Employee onboarding software comparison

The WEBSITE develops deep topical coverage.

But each PAGE remains focused on a specific problem.

This architecture also creates clearer internal-linking relationships and gives

LLMs have a natural tendency to expand.

Ask an AI system to "make this comprehensive" and it can continuously generate:

- more sections;
- more FAQs;
- more examples;
- more definitions;
- more related concepts.

Without a stopping rule, AI-assisted SEO can produce bloated pages.

Therefore, the final workflow needs both:

**a coverage test**

and

**a stopping rule.**

Content should continue expanding only while additional information materially
improves satisfaction of the target intent.


# Emerging Original Idea — The Expansion Test

Before adding another section to an AI-assisted article, ask:

### 1. Intent Test

Does this section directly help satisfy the primary or an essential supporting
intent?

### 2. Decision Test

Does it help the reader understand, evaluate, compare, decide, or act?

### 3. Information-Gain Test

Does it add something meaningful rather than restating information already
covered?

If the proposed section fails all three tests:

**DO NOT ADD IT.**

This is my current synthesis from Indig's focused-content findings and the
earlier experts' emphasis on topical coverage.

The formal Expansion Test was not found stated in this form in the reviewed
sources and is therefore being tracked as a potential original contribution.

## My Assessment

Brian Dean contributes something deceptively simple to this playbook:

**The reader still has to win.**

AI-search optimization creates a temptation to think primarily about:

- citations;
- entities;
- retrieval;
- embeddings;
- semantic coverage;
- heading structures;
- AI visibility.

Those things matter.

But a B2B SaaS article ultimately exists because a human has a problem,
question, comparison, or decision to make.

If the page is highly retrievable but unpleasant or unhelpful to consume, the
workflow has optimized the intermediary system while failing the actual user.

I therefore introduce a hierarchy:

**Human Utility**
        ↓
**Search / Retrieval Clarity**
        ↓
**AI Citation Readiness**

The layers should reinforce one another.

They should not be reversed.


# THE TWIST — The Human Utility Test

Before a draft moves into technical optimization, it must pass a Human Utility
Test.

The reviewer asks four questions:


### 1. Intent

Does this page actually solve the problem implied by the target query or
customer question?


### 2. Time-to-Value

How quickly can the reader reach the information they came for?


### 3. Decision Value

Does the content help the reader understand, compare, decide, or act?


### 4. Unique Value

Would the reader lose something meaningful if this page disappeared and they
had to rely on the existing competing pages?


If the answer to the fourth question is:

**"Not really"**

then the page requires additional differentiation before publication.


## Why the Human Utility Test Matters

AI can produce content that is:

grammatically correct,
well structured,
keyword relevant,
and semantically comprehensive,

while still being completely forgettable.

That is a dangerous failure mode because automated quality systems may rate the
draft highly even when a human reader gains little from it.

The Human Utility Test therefore evaluates something that conventional SEO
checklists can miss:

**Was this content actually worth creating?**


# Emerging Original Idea — Replace "AI Detection" With "AI Dependence"

A common question in AI-content production is:

**"Can Google detect that AI wrote this?"**

I believe that is the wrong operational question.

The better question is:

**"How dependent is this article on information that AI could have generated
without us?"**

I call this:

# AI Dependence

Consider two articles.


### Article A

Contains:

- public definitions;
- common statistics;
- competitor summaries;
- generic examples;
- LLM-generated recommendations.

AI Dependence: **HIGH**


### Article B

Contains:

- customer interview findings;
- internal product data;
- SME commentary;
- original examples;
- proprietary screenshots;
- company experiments;
- first-hand case studies.

AI Dependence: **LOW**


The objective is not necessarily to minimize the amount of text generated with
AI.

The objective is to minimize the amount of VALUE that depends exclusively on
generic AI knowledge.

This distinction allows AI to remain a powerful production tool without making
the final content interchangeable with thousands of other AI-generated pages.

Dean's semantic SEO guidance emphasizes comprehensive, high-quality information.

I therefore interpret Dean's recommendation as:

**Cover the information necessary to satisfy the topic and intent.**

I do NOT interpret it as:

Examples of Backlinko pages achieving strong rankings demonstrate that the

They do not establish that another B2B SaaS website will achieve the same

### Original Research Has a Cost

Original research is highly differentiated but expensive.

The final playbook therefore needs multiple levels of information gain rather

## What Brian Dean Contributes to the Final Playbook

At this stage, I would carry forward the following principles:

# THE TWIST — Every Page Needs a Job

Before creating a page, the brief must define:

# PAGE JOB

A page cannot enter production simply because:

**"We found a keyword."**

It needs a specific job inside the content system.


## Possible Page Jobs

### 1. DEFINE

Explain a concept the audience needs to understand.


### 2. SOLVE

Help the user solve a specific problem.


### 3. COMPARE

Help the user evaluate alternatives.


### 4. ENABLE

Provide a tool, template, calculator, checklist, or process that helps the user
perform a task.


### 5. PROVE

Provide evidence through a case study, benchmark, original research, or
customer result.


### 6. CONVERT

Help a qualified visitor evaluate or purchase the product.


### 7. SUPPORT

Strengthen another strategically important page through relevant knowledge and
internal relationships.


## Why This Matters

Without a Page Job, AI-assisted content production can create pages that are:

relevant,
optimized,
well written,

but strategically unnecessary.

The Page Job forces the team to explain the role of the asset before investing
in production.


# Emerging Original Idea — The Page Contract

I extend the Page Job into what I call a:

# PAGE CONTRACT

Before drafting begins, every page receives a short contract containing five
items:


### 1. Audience

Who specifically is this page for?


### 2. Job

What job must the page perform?


### 3. Intent

What does the visitor need when they arrive?


### 4. Contribution

What unique information or functionality will we contribute?


### 5. Next Relationship

Where should the user or information graph logically move after this page?


## Example

**Page:** Employee Onboarding Checklist

**Audience:** HR managers implementing a new onboarding process.

**Job:** ENABLE

**Intent:** Obtain a practical onboarding checklist that can be used
immediately.

**Contribution:** Checklist built from internal onboarding specialists,
customer implementation data, and recurring onboarding mistakes.

**Next Relationship:** Link naturally toward onboarding automation,
implementation guidance, and relevant product functionality.


## Why I Prefer This to a Keyword-Only Brief

A keyword tells us:

**what someone typed.**

The Page Contract tells us:

**why this page deserves a place in the system.**

The company deliberately creates or coordinates apparently separate documents

# My Assessment

Ahrefs changes the playbook less through a new SEO tactic and more through a
new research principle:

# Do Not Overfit the SOP to Today's AI Behavior.

This is extremely important.

SEO practitioners naturally want actionable rules.

AI-search research produces numbers that look like rules:

- citation percentages;
- ranking overlap;
- word-count ranges;
- mention correlations;
- platform citation rates.

But Ahrefs' own research demonstrates that these numbers can move substantially
as:

- models change;
- retrieval systems change;
- interfaces change;
- methodologies improve;
- user behavior changes.

The playbook therefore needs two classes of evidence.


# THE TWIST — Durable vs Volatile Rules

Every major AI-search recommendation should be classified as either:


## DURABLE

Likely to remain useful even if individual platforms change.

Examples:

- satisfy user intent;
- maintain crawlability;
- provide accurate information;
- use relevant internal links;
- contribute first-hand evidence;
- maintain content freshness;
- build legitimate authority;
- structure information clearly.


## VOLATILE

Dependent on the current behavior of a particular system.

Examples:

- current citation-overlap percentages;
- exact optimal word-count ranges;
- current prompt patterns;
- exact citation-position correlations;
- current platform referral rates;
- current AI crawler behavior.


## Operational Rule

**Durable findings can become SOP rules.**

**Volatile findings become monitoring variables.**

This prevents the playbook from becoming obsolete every time an AI platform
changes its retrieval behavior.


# THE TWIST — Maintenance Needs Its Own Decision System

The earlier workflow had:

Measure
↓
Refresh / Redistribute

Ahrefs shows that this is too simplistic.

After publication, a page can have several different problems:

- outdated information;
- weak distribution;
- search-intent drift;
- cannibalization;
- low quality;
- declining rankings;
- broken links;
- strong traffic but weak conversion;
- low traffic but high sales value.

Those problems require different actions.


# Emerging Original Idea — The 5R Maintenance Decision

After a page has accumulated enough performance data, choose among five actions:


## 1. RETAIN

Use when:

- information remains accurate;
- the page fulfills its job;
- performance is acceptable;
- no major strategic change is required.

Action:

**Leave the core asset alone and continue monitoring.**


## 2. REFRESH

Use when:

- information is outdated;
- product details changed;
- competitors changed;
- statistics are stale;
- intent evolved;
- useful sections are missing.

Action:

**Update the asset while preserving its successful foundation.**


## 3. REDISTRIBUTE

Use when:

- the asset remains strong;
- information is current;
- performance is limited mainly by insufficient exposure.

Action:

**Reshare, repost, remix, activate through sales, email, communities, or other
relevant channels.**


## 4. RECONSOLIDATE

Use when:

- multiple pages overlap heavily;
- cannibalization exists;
- several weak pages could become one stronger asset;
- site architecture has become unnecessarily fragmented.

Action:

**Merge strategically and redirect where appropriate.**


## 5. RETIRE

Use when:

- the content is no longer relevant;
- it provides no meaningful traffic, links, sales, historical, or user value;
- updating it is not economically justified.

Action:

**Remove or redirect it carefully.**


## Why This Matters

"Refresh old content" is too vague to function as an SOP.

The 5R system forces the team to diagnose the problem before choosing the
maintenance action.


## Source of This Idea

Ahrefs' content-pruning and freshness research informs this framework.

Ross Simmonds' redistribution research contributes the redistribution option.

The formal five-part:

**Retain / Refresh / Redistribute / Reconsolidate / Retire**

decision system was not found stated in this form in the reviewed sources.

It is therefore being tracked as a potential original contribution.

# Emerging Original Idea — Visibility-to-Value Ladder

I introduce a measurement model called the:

# VISIBILITY-TO-VALUE LADDER


## Level 1 — Discoverability

Can the content be found?

Measure:

- rankings;
- impressions;
- indexed visibility.


## Level 2 — AI Presence

Does the brand/content appear inside AI-mediated discovery?

Measure:

- mentions;
- citations;
- cited pages;
- prompt coverage.


## Level 3 — Attention

Does the visibility produce meaningful human engagement?

Measure:

- visits;
- engaged sessions;
- reading / interaction;
- tool usage.


## Level 4 — Progression

Does the content move the user toward another meaningful step?

Measure:

- internal progression;
- product-page visits;
- comparison behavior;
- email signup;
- template download.


## Level 5 — Commercial Value

Does the content influence business outcomes?

Measure:

- qualified leads;
- demos;
- trials;
- pipeline;
- revenue.


## Operational Rule

Do not diagnose performance from one level alone.

A problem at each level requires a different response.

For example:

**High Discoverability + Low Attention**

Possible problem:
poor SERP click-through or AI answers satisfying the query without a click.


**High Attention + Low Progression**

Possible problem:
weak CTA, wrong audience, or poor journey design.


**High Progression + Low Commercial Value**

Possible problem:
lead quality, product-market fit, pricing, sales process, or attribution.

# WHAT I REJECTED — CANDIDATE 3

## Special GEO Technical Requirements Without Platform Evidence

A growing number of AI-search discussions suggest that websites need entirely
new technical mechanisms specifically to become eligible for AI-generated
search results.

Examples can include claims that publishers must add:

- special AI schema;
- special AI markup;
- special machine-readable AI files;
- new technical artifacts purely for AI visibility.


## Why I Reject This as a Mandatory SOP Requirement

Google explicitly states that no special schema.org markup or special AI text
file is required for inclusion in AI Overviews or AI Mode.

This does not mean experimental technical standards can never become useful.

It means:

# Experimental ≠ Required.


## Decision

**REJECT AS A MANDATORY REQUIREMENT.**

If future evidence demonstrates value from an emerging standard, it can be
tested.

It should not enter the core SOP merely because the AI-SEO industry is
discussing it.


# WHAT I REJECTED — CANDIDATE 4

## Arbitrary SEO Word Counts

The idea:

**Every SEO article should be at least X words.**


## Why I Reject It

Google explicitly says it does not have a preferred word count.

Kevin Indig's empirical work also challenges the assumption that maximum page
coverage produces maximum AI citation performance.

Brian Dean's comprehensiveness principle is better interpreted as:

**fully satisfy the user's need**

rather than:

**maximize length.**


## Decision

**REJECT.**

Page length will follow:

- intent;
- Page Job;
- evidence requirements;
- complexity of the problem.

Not an arbitrary SEO number.


# Final Source-Skepticism Note

Official documentation deserves high evidentiary weight for:

- platform policies;
- eligibility;
- supported technical controls;
- official requirements.

But official documentation still requires critical interpretation.


## Google Has Commercial Incentives

Google benefits when publishers continue participating in its search ecosystem.

Claims about:

- traffic quality;
- publisher opportunity;
- user satisfaction;

should therefore not automatically be treated as independent business evidence.


## Google Does Not Reveal Its Complete Ranking Algorithm

Official guidance does not tell us the exact weights or mechanisms behind every
ranking decision.

Therefore:

Google saying:

**"Create people-first content"**

does not tell us exactly how Google's systems mathematically evaluate every
page.


## Policy Is Not Prediction

Following Google's guidance reduces policy risk.

It does not guarantee:

- indexing;
- ranking;
- AI citation;
- traffic;
- conversion.

Google explicitly acknowledges that meeting its requirements does not guarantee
that a page will be crawled, indexed, or served.


# What Google Contributes to the Final Playbook

I carry forward these principles:

1. Preserve conventional SEO fundamentals.
2. Do not invent a separate mandatory GEO technical stack.
3. Keep important content crawlable, indexable, and internally discoverable.
4. Produce helpful, reliable, people-first content.
5. Prefer original and non-commodity information.
6. Use AI as an assistant rather than a justification for low-value scale.
7. Do not generate pages for every possible query variation.
8. Maintain accuracy, quality, and relevance.
9. Keep structured data consistent with visible content.
10. Do not use arbitrary word-count targets.
11. Do not fake freshness.
12. Communicate authorship appropriately.
13. Explain substantial automation when that context benefits the reader.
14. Treat E-E-A-T as a quality concept, not a numeric ranking score.
15. Do not treat quality-rater guidelines as Google's ranking algorithm.
16. Preserve human judgment.
17. Give every mandatory workflow step a Complexity Budget.
18. Require meaningful Human Value Delta from strategically important assets.
